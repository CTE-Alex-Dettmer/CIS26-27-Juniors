Computer Systems Manager

E.T. GO HOME!

Computer Systems Managers, or CSMs, sit at the top of the information technology chain, monitoring networks and data
processing systems, managing funds and the hardware those funds go into maintaining, and all the while coaching their
colleagues. Like any other IT job, there will be more than plenty of ιd1φt errors, but that's simply the ups and downs of
the trade.

What Do They Do?

CSMs' tasks can be split into three categories:

  • Hardware Tasks (upgrades, repairs, maintenance)
  • Software Tasks (OS updates, internal errors, failures to boot)
  • Social Tasks (coaching colleagues, coordinating projects, reviewing budgets)
Each one is important in different ways and at different times, but all three are necessary to be performed.

Needed Skills

Technical Skills

  • Critical Thinking
    Things are going to go wrong. That much is just a constant when it comes to computers. Someone will install
    something, update something, delete something, or make a change without approval, or the machine will simply
    hit the fan. And most likely, in the event of human error, they will refuse to admit what they did wrong. Which
    means it's up to you to figure it out on your own. This is why critical thinking and problem solving are so crucial:
    because finding the problem is important, but knowing how to fix it is just as important, if not more.

  • Systems Knowledge
    While it's great to be able to figure out a problem by chucking error codes into ChatGPT, you should be able to
    diagnose the problem on your own. This requires that you actually know the system that's hit the fan. A doctor
    can't simply tell Gemini, "the patient's throwing up sometimes, what do I do" because the patient can tell Gemini
    that themselves. The doctor has to be able to diagnose it themselves, or they're out of a job. This doesn't mean
    you have to know every single error code, but you should know what's broken when something clearly is.

  • Resource Management
    This one is half technical, half people skill. Even as the manager of computer systems, you won't have infinite
    funds or resources, because you can't bankrupt your employers for a 5090. Resources aren't only money, they're
    internal machine specs, too. You can only run so many things on a machine at any given moment before your
    hardware reaches its limit. At that point, you can either use funds to upgrade, or spread the workload across
    different machines. See? This ties into problem-solving, too.

Soft Skills (People Skills)

  • Leadership/Staff Management
    As the computer systems manager, you have a fair few subordinates to coach, assist, take input from, and be
    assisted by. This means you need to be able to run a team. Just running Teams takes 61.8 MB of RAM, but
    running a group of people collaborating, communicating, and working together takes a lot more. It takes
    technical skills, sure, but it also requires patience, caution, situational awareness, and above all, it requires...

  • Communication Skills
    No one likes having to talk to people, but as a CSM, that is literally half your job. You talk to higher-ups,
    lower-ups, and people on your level to communicate upcoming plans, current problems, and answer questions,
    all without making them feel stupid. That's the important part. I know for a fact that a good chunk of my father's
    day is spent in a Zoom call, with someone else's screen pulled up. And while he's not always doing the talking,
    he's almost always answering questions when he does.

Four Steps to (possible) Success

I say possible success because nothing will always go your way, every time. You might have to try several times, get
more certifications than you thought you needed, because the employer you shot for didn't specify which ones they
wanted you to have. But that's why they let you apply several times, anyway.

  1. Get a Bachelor's Degree
    This is the first certification you require to even have a shot at getting this job, and it specifically needs to be
    in computer/information science, or data science. They also require you to have general work experience,
    since this is not an entry-level job. Bachelor's degrees require 120 college credits across four years of actual
    college, which requires that you either receive 15 college credits in each of your eight semesters. This can
    fluctuate however it needs to, but you have to get 120 at the end of your four years.

  2. Actually Gain Work Experience
    As previously referenced, you can't just walk in on your first day out of college and expect to be picked up as
    a CSM. Entry-level jobs even require 5 years of previous work experience (which doesn't make sense for an
    entry-level job, but I digress), so it stands to reason that a high-level job like a CSM is going to require that
    minimum as well. Luckily, many colleges, such as SUNY Canton, do actually provide on-campus jobs for you
    to actually receive work experience. Should you begin this kind of program in your senior year of high school,
    you can walk out of college after your fourth year with the required five years.

  3. Gain/Have Leadership Existence
    This one is tough to do. Gaining leadership experience is difficult when you are far from a position of
    leadership. However, there are opportunities. Accept requests to manage a small project, or lead a sub-team in
    a part of software being launched. Nothing is too small, but you have to actually seek out the opportunities.

  4. Apply for the Position
    Finally, something straightforward. Apply for the spot, sell yourself well in the interview, and hope all goes well.
    If it doesn't? That's okay. Wait a few months, improve yourself, and apply again. The worst they can say is no,
    and you can always try again.
